Two jobs are yours. Do those well.

Contents
    Job one · supply

    Choosing the manufacturer

    Supply failure ended this business once. The whole point of private labelling is to hand that risk to someone with real quality systems — which only works if you actually check that they have them.

    The enquiry email — send this to all six

    Ask everything at once. A supplier who answers all of it clearly is already telling you something useful about how they operate.

    • Price per kg and per finished labelled unit, at 25 kg / 50 kg / 100 kg
    • MOQ per flavour, and whether a combined minimum can be split across two flavours
    • Lead time from order confirmation to dispatch
    • A paid sample of each flavour — say you'll pay, it gets taken seriously
    • Copy of their FSSAI licence, and confirmation it covers this product category
    • Spec sheet and COA: is the flavouring natural, nature-identical or artificial? This is the question most likely to be dodged, and it's the one your label depends on.
    • Do they supply the jar or pouch, or do you? Do they apply your label?
    • Stated shelf life, and the storage conditions it assumes
    • Can they repeat the identical profile in three and six months?
    • Do they provide a nutritional analysis you can legally print?
    The two-quote trick

    Ask every supplier for both a finished-and-labelled price and a bulk unbranded price. One listing suggests finished private-label jars can work out cheaper per kilo than raw bulk from other suppliers — which would invert the usual assumption. It's one data point and may not survive real quotes, but you'll only find out by asking both ways.

    Judging the samples

    Taste blind, and taste the way your customer will drink it — which for Indian instant coffee means mostly with milk and sugar, not black. A flavour that reads as pleasant black can vanish entirely in milk, and a flavour that's cloying black can be right in a latte. Test both, at the dose printed on the pack.

    What separates a good sample from a bad one: does the flavour taste like the thing it claims, or like a generic sweet note? Is there a chemical aftertaste? Does it dissolve cleanly in hot and cold water? Cold matters — a lot of Indian instant coffee is drunk as cold coffee.

    The four-week test, before you commit the budget

    Instant coffee is hygroscopic — it pulls moisture from the air and then clumps, cakes into a solid block, and loses aroma. A jar that was fine on arrival can be a brick two months later, and the customer reads that as a cheap product. If your 2023 quality complaints were about clumping or coffee going stale fast, this was the cause.

    So before the full run: keep two sample jars sealed at room temperature, two somewhere warm and humid, and open and reclose two daily as a customer would. At four weeks check all six for caking, aroma and taste. It costs nothing and it is exactly the step that got skipped last time.

    Job one · the part with legal weight

    You carry their liability

    What "deemed manufacturer" means

    As a Relabeller, FSSAI treats you as the manufacturer of a product you did not make. If something goes wrong, the liability attaches to Buzzcaf, not to the factory. You are responsible for Schedule 4 hygiene compliance at their premises. This is why the checks below are not paperwork — they're the only thing standing between you and someone else's bad batch.

    Before you sign anything

    • Verify the FSSAI licence is valid, current, and covers this product category. Check the number on FoSCoS yourself rather than trusting a PDF.
    • Ask for recent third-party lab test reports, and any record of FSSAI inspections, notices or suspensions.
    • Check corporate registration and how long they've actually traded.
    • Ask directly how they handle a batch that fails, and listen for whether the answer sounds practised or invented.
    • Visit the front-runner if you can reach them. Indore, Surat and Kushalnagar are all a day's travel from Pune. You are legally answerable for that facility.

    Put in the agreement

    • Exclusive use of your brand name — they may not sell your recipe or artwork to anyone else
    • Who supplies and pays for packaging, and who owns unused stock of it
    • A COA with every batch, not on request
    • What happens to a defective batch: replacement, credit, and who pays return freight
    • Batch coding and traceability — you need to trace any unit back to a production lot
    • Delivery timelines with a stated remedy for lateness
    • Notice period on termination, and confidentiality

    On every delivery, without exception

    Check the paperwork firstCOA present, batch number legible, manufacture and best-before dates correct and consistent with the label.
    Open a unit from the batchAroma, colour, free-flowing texture, no clumps. Brew a cup, hot and cold.
    Check the label against your artworkBoth FSSAI numbers, net quantity, MRP, batch, dates. A wrong label is a recall, not a reprint.
    Check fill weight and sealWeigh a few. Confirm the induction seal is intact — a failed seal becomes a caked jar and a one-star review.
    Keep two retention samples per batchSealed, labelled, stored past their best-before. If a customer complains later, these settle it.
    Log itBatch, date, quantity, supplier, COA reference. This is your recall trail and your annual return evidence.

    Only after all six does stock go to Amazon. A bad batch discovered at FBA is expensive to remove and can damage account health — the account being the most valuable thing you own.

    Job one · artwork

    The label, which now names two companies

    Your old labels said Buzzcaf manufactured and packed the product. That is no longer true, so they need reprinting. Both parties now appear:

    The convention

    Manufactured by: [manufacturer's name, full address, their FSSAI licence number]
    Marketed by: Buzzcaf Private Limited, [your licensed address], FSSAI 11522079000056

    Everything else stays as the law requires. Check artwork against this list before it goes to press, and again on the printer's proof:

    • Name of the food, and ingredients in descending order by weight — including the flavouring, described correctly as natural, nature-identical or artificial
    • Nutritional information per 100 g — from an actual lab analysis, not copied from a competitor. Ask whether the manufacturer's report covers you.
    • Green veg symbol: filled circle in a green-outlined square
    • Allergen declaration if applicable
    • FSSAI logo and your licence number, in a contrasting colour
    • Net quantity, and MRP inclusive of all taxes
    • Consumer care — a phone number and email that are monitored
    • Batch number, manufacture date, best before — DD/MM/YYYY
    • Storage instructions. For instant coffee, say something useful: keep tightly closed, use a dry spoon.
    • Minimum 3 mm font for Schedule II declarations
    Also required on the listing page

    Since the 2023 Legal Metrology amendment, the Amazon listing itself must carry manufacturer name and address, generic name, net quantity, MRP inclusive of taxes, consumer care details and country of origin — before the physical pack reaches the buyer. Build these into the listing when you create it, not afterwards. A duo pack is a multi-piece package and needs its own declarations including piece count and total net quantity.

    Job two · where the money is made

    The Amazon listing

    With no ad budget, the listing is the marketing. This is the highest-leverage work in the plan and it costs nothing but your hours. Spend them here before anywhere else.

    Enrol in Brand Registry first

    Your trademark is registered, so you get the full set immediately: A+ content, a Brand Store, Sponsored Brands, Brand Analytics, and IP protection. A+ content alone is worth doing properly — Amazon's own guidance attributes meaningful conversion lift to it.

    Seven images, each with a job

    2Flavour infographicNames the flavour boldly. Most instant coffee thumbnails are unreadable at phone size — being legible is a genuine edge.

    Title, written for search

    The title is your ranking. Lead with category and flavour, because that's what people type — "hazelnut instant coffee", not "Buzzcaf Desire". Include format, size and a qualifier. Put the brand where it belongs for a brand nobody is searching for yet: present, but not first.

    Reviews are the gate

    Below a solid base of genuine reviews, traffic will not convert and advertising is money set on fire. Getting there:

    • Use "Request a Review" on every single order. It's built into Seller Central, it's compliant, and almost nobody does it consistently.
    • Never incentivise a review, never insert a card asking for five stars, never ask friends to buy and review. It is against policy and it risks the account you just recovered — which is worth more than any launch.
    • Answer every question on the listing yourself, quickly. It converts browsers and it signals an attentive seller.
    • Respond to negative reviews plainly and fix what they identify. Early reviews tell you what's wrong while it's still cheap to change.

    Then, and only then, advertise

    Once reviews are in place, start small on Sponsored Products: your own brand terms plus close category terms like "flavoured instant coffee" and "hazelnut coffee powder". Judge it on whether it produces profitable orders, not on impressions.

    Job two · logistics you don't touch

    FBA mechanics

    • Confirm the Retailer KOB is on your FSSAI licence before you create a shipment. Amazon requires it for FBA food eligibility, and discovering this at the warehouse door costs you weeks.
    • Ship direct from the manufacturer to the fulfilment centre where possible. Saves a leg of freight and a handling step.
    • Amazon enforces remaining-shelf-life rules on food. Confirm the current requirement in Seller Central before shipping — stock arriving too close to its best-before can be rejected, and you'd be paying to have it returned.
    • Allow real time for receipting. It is slower than you expect, it's outside your control, and it's the step most likely to make you miss a seasonal window.
    • Watch storage fees if stock moves slowly. Send a first tranche rather than everything at once.
    • Never go out of stock. On Amazon this costs search ranking that takes weeks to rebuild — often more damaging than the lost sales. Reorder at a threshold, not when the shelf is empty.
    Supporting · one weekend

    The website

    First, recover the domains

    buzzcaf.in fails to resolve at DNS level. Your FSSAI licence lists sudhansu@buzzcaf.com, so you may have held the .com as well. Check both registrars today. Re-register whatever has lapsed before you spend an hour on anything else here.

    The site is not your sales engine — Amazon is. It's the page someone opens after seeing your jar, to decide whether you're a real company. Build it accordingly and then leave it alone.

    Supporting · one hour a week

    Instagram, with realistic expectations

    With 66 followers and no budget, Instagram is not going to drive sales. Its job is to look alive when someone checks whether you're real. Judge it on that and it becomes easy to sustain.

    • Revive @buzzcaf. Don't start a new handle — account age and history beat a clean slate.
    • Three posts a week, shot in one batch session. Ten hours a week doesn't survive daily content.
    • Recipes are your best content — cold coffee, dalgona, mocha, iced latte. They're useful, they're searched, they show the product in use, and they double as listing images and website content. One shoot, three uses.
    • Show the real thing: boxes arriving, jars being checked, orders going out. Process content is the most credible thing a small food brand can post, and you shoot for a living.
    • Set up a Google Business Profile too. Free, twenty minutes, and it makes the company findable and legitimate-looking.
